# Occupational Health of Women Agricultural Workers in India

## Summary
Women form a substantial share of India's agricultural labour force, including in sugarcane cultivation, yet occupational health infrastructure, protective equipment, and tool design remain largely unadapted to their needs. A peer-reviewed review published via PubMed Central (PMC) synthesizes the occupational health issues facing Indian women agricultural workers and proposes concrete policy and workplace interventions.

## Main Information
- Women agricultural workers in India face a distinct set of occupational health risks tied to manual field labour, often compounded by a lack of access to appropriately designed protective equipment and tools.
- India's constitutional framework provides baseline worker protections relevant to this population: Article 24 prohibits employment of children under 14 years; Article 39(e and f) states that the health of men, women, and children must be protected and that children should have opportunities for healthy development, free from exploitation; Article 42 provides for humane working conditions and maternity relief.
- Complementary research (a structured survey of 500 female farm labourers across five major agricultural states — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, Punjab, and Tamil Nadu) drew on secondary data from ICMR, WHO, and ILO to assess occupational health challenges among women farm workers, providing a broader empirical base for the issues summarized here.
- India's National Institute of Occupational Health (NIOH), an ICMR institute established in 1970 as a WHO Collaborating Centre for occupational health, works closely with the Ministries of Labour, Health and Family Welfare, Environment and Forests, and Agriculture; **women's health is explicitly listed among NIOH's thrust research areas**, alongside occupational/environmental epidemiology, toxicology, and agricultural health. NIOH has a Regional Occupational Health Centre in Bengaluru, Karnataka (established 1977), placing dedicated occupational-health research capacity within the same state as this project's target sugarcane-growing region.

## Important Recommendations (per the source review)
1. Occupational health in agriculture must be addressed through a well-planned strategy integrated with rural development programs, backed by national policy support.
2. Health education and awareness should be provided specifically to women workers, enabling them to organize collectively to advocate for improved working conditions.
3. Human resources — doctors, paramedical personnel, physiotherapists, social workers, and counsellors — should receive dedicated training and retraining in occupational health relevant to agricultural settings.
4. Government policy should make it **mandatory for employers to provide safety equipment** — proper footwear, gloves, masks, and head covering — which the review identifies as capable of substantially reducing occupational health issues among women workers.
5. Tools and equipment should be redesigned according to the physical body postures and ergonomic needs of women workers, and this redesign should be pursued in a cost-effective manner so it is actually adoptable at the farm level.
6. Premier academic and research institutions should introduce dedicated coursework on occupational health in agriculture, with government support, to build long-term technical capacity in this area.

## Key Facts
- NIOH (ICMR), the national nodal institute for occupational health research in India, explicitly designates women's health as one of its thrust research areas alongside agricultural health.
- NIOH has a dedicated Regional Occupational Health Centre in Bengaluru, Karnataka, established in 1977.
- A cross-state Indian survey specifically covering female farm labourers (n=500) spanned five major agricultural states, drawing on ICMR, WHO, and ILO secondary data.
