# Occupational Hazards in Sugarcane Cultivation and Harvesting

## Summary
Sugarcane farming is labour-intensive, and harvesting is consistently identified in peer-reviewed literature as its single most hazardous activity, associated with cuts, musculoskeletal disorders, and pesticide-related respiratory and dermatological problems. This document synthesizes findings from a systematic review of occupational hazards among sugarcane farm workers, published in an agricultural sciences journal, for use in labour-safety advisory content.

## Main Information
- Sugarcane is cultivated globally as a critical cash crop, with India ranked as the second-largest producer after Brazil; however, workers in developing countries such as India face significant work-related health issues, including ergonomic problems, occupational hazards, and injuries specifically tied to labour-intensive cultivation practices.
- **Harvesting is identified as the most hazardous activity** in the sugarcane production cycle. Repetitive cutting movements combined with poorly designed hand tools frequently lead to cuts, injuries, and musculoskeletal disorders among manual harvesters.
- **Pesticide exposure** is a separate, significant occupational hazard: workers are exposed to high levels of toxic pesticides during application and field work, resulting in respiratory issues such as coughing and chest pain, and — per related literature on sugarcane workers in Maharashtra — ophthalmological and dermatological problems linked specifically to pesticide exposure.
- A broader systematic literature review of sugarcane agro-industrial health and safety additionally found that the manual cane-cutting process, especially when combined with pre-harvest burning of the crop, poses substantial risk to workers during the harvesting phase — a practice pattern also noted in India's own IISR post-harvest documentation (see the Post-Harvest Management file), which records that some regions still practise pre-crushing cane burning near the end of the crushing season.
- Heat exposure during manual harvesting is a further documented risk internationally, contributing to dehydration, heat exhaustion, and — in severe, prolonged-exposure occupational settings — kidney injury among heat-exposed sugarcane workers, reinforcing the need for hydration and rest-break provisions during harvest-season field work.

## Important Recommendations
- Provide manual harvesters with properly designed, well-maintained cutting tools rather than generic implements, since poorly designed tools are specifically identified as a contributing factor to cuts and musculoskeletal injury (not simply the cutting task itself).
- Where pesticide application is part of a farm worker's role, basic personal protective measures (covering skin, avoiding inhalation exposure, washing after application) should be actively promoted, given the documented link between pesticide exposure and respiratory, ophthalmological, and dermatological problems among sugarcane workers.
- During harvest-season field work in hot conditions, encourage hydration breaks and shaded rest periods for manual labourers, consistent with the international evidence on heat-related occupational illness among sugarcane harvesters.
- Discourage pre-harvest burning of standing cane where avoidable, both because it accelerates sucrose loss (see Post-Harvest Management document) and because it is independently associated with elevated worker health risk during the cutting process.

## Key Facts
- Harvesting is the single most hazardous stage of sugarcane cultivation, per systematic review evidence.
- Documented worker health impacts include: cuts and musculoskeletal disorders (from harvesting tools/technique), respiratory issues (pesticide exposure), and ophthalmological/dermatological problems (pesticide exposure, per Maharashtra-focused sugarcane worker studies).
- Heat-related illness (dehydration, heat exhaustion, and in severe cases kidney injury) is a documented risk for manual sugarcane harvesters, particularly relevant in India's hot pre-monsoon harvesting window.
